How a claim enters the catalogue

The catalogue uses primary sources. Every claim on an entry carries a link to the source that establishes it, and a search result or a secondary article can point research at that source but never stands in for it.

A citation names the smallest part of its source that states the claim: a specification section, a release-note heading, a heading in the proposal text itself. A source that can change after it is read — meeting notes, a proposal still being edited, compiler source — is cited at a fixed revision instead, so the link keeps showing what was read rather than what the page says now.

Proposal advancement, annual publication, a compiler release and runtime availability are four facts with four sources, and nothing about one of them implies another. A proposal can be standardized with no edition carrying it yet, a published feature need not run in any engine, and a shipped compiler release settles nothing about a standard.

That is why 188 entries sit in three streams whose dates mean different things, and why coverage is reported for each part of the record rather than as one date for everything.

Source precedence, stream by stream

Two sources can speak to one claim, and one of them has to win. Each stream resolves that in a fixed order, highest authority first, and each step names the body or the document that decides.

ECMAScript

  1. TC39's list of finished proposals settles that a proposal reached Stage 4, and the annual edition it is expected in.
  2. TC39's table of active proposals settles a stage a proposal is still moving through, and that table is read at a fixed revision, so a stage stated here is the one that was read.
  3. A proposal's own text settles what the feature does, how it behaves, and what it was called before.
  4. Once an annual edition is published, the ECMA-262 text of that edition outranks the proposal's text wherever the two differ.
  5. TC39 meeting notes settle when a decision was taken: an advancement, a split, a merge or a rename.
  6. Ecma International's release material settles that an edition was approved and published.

Where it splitsStage 4 and an annual edition are two facts with two dates, and neither is inferred from the other. 4 entries have reached Stage 4 with no publication date recorded, and one — Temporal — has no settled edition at all.

TypeScript

  1. A stable release settles whether a version shipped and on what date.
  2. That version's release announcement settles what changed for the person writing code.
  3. The handbook and the compiler-option reference settle what the behavior is now.
  4. A pull request or an issue is read only where the release material is incomplete or disagrees with the shipped behavior.

Where it splitsA release page can be rewritten after the fact. Where its date and the stable announcement disagree, the entry cites both, the catalogue takes the date the cited evidence establishes, and the disagreement is held once for that version instead of being repeated on every entry the version carries.

Runtime

  1. Documentation and release notes from the engine or platform itself — Node.js, V8, WebKit, Firefox — settle when a feature became usable there.
  2. Browser compatibility data from MDN settles the first version of an engine that shipped it.
  3. Each availability claim carries its own source and the date that source was read, one claim per engine and version.

Where it splitsA proposal stage or a published specification never establishes support. An availability claim is added only after a source for that exact engine and version is found, so an unverified one is left out rather than estimated, and a runtime entry exists only where availability decides whether a feature can be used at all.

The rules, and the catalogue today

These are the rules a claim is admitted under. The note beneath a rule is what the catalogue looks like against it today.

  • Stage advancement and edition publication are independent facts.
  • An entry counts as standardized only with Stage 4 evidence or a published specification behind it.In the catalogue81 entries are recorded as standardized.
  • Stable TypeScript behavior needs a stable release. A beta or a release candidate establishes prerelease status and nothing further.In the catalogue0 entries are recorded as a release candidate, so nothing in the catalogue rests on prerelease behavior.
  • A renamed, split or merged proposal stays one entry, keeps the names it used to have, and cites the meeting notes for the change.In the catalogue80 entries carry at least one former name.
  • Primary sources that disagree are both kept on the entry rather than settled by preference.In the catalogue1 entry is recorded as disputed today: Temporal, whose edition the catalogue also records as unresolved. The separate Temporal runtime availability entry records where that API is available, which is why the two facts are never merged into one.
  • A fact that cannot be settled stays marked unverified until a primary source settles it.In the catalogue0 entries are waiting on verification.
  • Every source is recorded with a descriptive title and the date it was read.
  • A long passage from a source is paraphrased, and every example is written for this catalogue.

Sources the catalogue never cites

A search result or a secondary article can send research towards a primary source. Neither is evidence, so neither is cited on an entry.

The catalogue does not cite personal blogs, Medium, dev.to, newsletters, tutorials, aggregators, AI-generated wikis, Reddit, Stack Overflow or social media.

How far the record has been reviewed

Coverage is not one date. The record is reviewed in parts, and reviewing one part never advances another: a pass covers a single part and at most one calendar year, and a pass that finds no change still says so before the reviewed date moves. Each part also has an earliest date in scope, which is a decision rather than a limit of the evidence: nothing before it has been reviewed.

Corrections

Every claim on an entry carries a link to the source that establishes it, at the smallest part of that source that states it. That is what a reader can do about a wrong claim: follow the link and compare it with the sentence, and the disagreement is visible without taking anything here on trust. There is no issue tracker and no contribution guide to route a report through, so a correction is a change to the entry itself.

A claim that no permitted source supports is dropped rather than softened, and a fact two primary sources disagree about keeps both positions on the entry instead of one preferred answer. An unsettled fact stays marked unverified until a primary source settles it, so what is still open is visible on the entries themselves.
